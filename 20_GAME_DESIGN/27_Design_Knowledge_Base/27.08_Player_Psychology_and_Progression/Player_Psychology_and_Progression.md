# 27.08 · Player psychology and felt progression

*New card (ARC-0016, Owner decision D6). Readers: Lead Class Designer, Spell Designer, Balance Designer, Lore Writer.
`21.04/_SUBCLASS_STANDARD.md` and `21.04/_SUBCLASS_REFINING.md` cite this card for the "why"; the questions themselves
live in the standard. Every source is in `27.07` with what was actually checked — most were verified online, not read
in full, so treat each idea as a lens until a playtest (`27.06`) confirms it for the Arcana.*

## Motivation — why a player keeps playing a class

- **Autonomy, competence, relatedness** (self-determination theory) each predict enjoyment and the wish to play again
  (Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski, 2006). `27.01` holds the class-design checks. For progression the key word is
  **competence**: the player has to *notice* that they got better, and the feedback has to say so.
- **Players want different things.** Quantic Foundry's survey model (Nick Yee) sorts gamer motivations into twelve.
  A subclass doesn't need to serve all of them; it should know which ones it serves and not punish the others.

## Flow and learning — how fast new things may arrive

- **Flow:** absorption happens when the challenge keeps pace with the player's growing skill (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990).
  A class that jumps in complexity breaks it; a class that never grows bores.
- **Cognitive load:** working memory is small — about three to five chunks (Cowan, 2001) — and new problems compete
  with learning for it (Sweller, 1988). *For class design:* count the **new concepts per level-up**; spread them;
  teach a rule only when the player can use it; keep the core rule one sentence so it costs one chunk.
- **Simple surface, deep play.** Rosewater's *lenticular design* (2014): one card reads simply to a new player and
  deeply to an expert. *New World Order* (2011): complexity creep — the gap between what a new player knows and what
  the game asks — drove new players away; complexity on the board counts more than complexity on one card.

## Decisions — what makes a turn interesting

- "A game is a series of interesting decisions" (Sid Meier, GDC 2012). A decision with one right answer every time is
  not a decision. *For class design:* hunt the **dominant option**; give every resource at least two good uses.

## Framing — the same numbers can feel like a gift or a fine

- **Loss aversion:** losses weigh more than equal gains; the value curve is steeper for losses (Kahneman and Tversky,
  1979).
- **The rested-XP case:** World of Warcraft's beta cut experience after hours of play; testers hated it. The live game
  gave a bonus for rested time instead — Blizzard described it as the same numbers seen from the other side (reported
  by Madigan, 2010, citing Gamasutra; community wiki history). *For class design:* a cost should read as a **trade the
  player chose**, not as something taken away. A mechanic that removes the player's turn is felt as punishment even when
  the arithmetic is fair.

## Memory of an experience — peaks and endings

- **Peak-end:** people judge a past experience mostly by its most intense moment and its end, and barely by its length
  (Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier, 1993). *For class design:* the journey's peak (level 12) has to
  **happen** in a typical fight, and a fight should be able to end on a high.

## Pull toward a goal — visible progress

- **Goal gradient:** effort speeds up as the goal gets closer (Hull, 1932; in people, Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, 2006
  — even an illusion of progress works).
- **Endowed progress:** a head start on the same goal raises completion (Nunes and Drèze, 2006).
- *For class design:* meters and Awakening goals should **show** how close they are; a counter the player can't see
  pulls no one.

## Systems — loops that run away

- Positive feedback amplifies and destabilises; negative feedback stabilises (Adams and Dormans, 2012). `27.04` holds the
  engine trap. *For class design:* every positive loop gets a brake (per turn, per chain, a cap, or a net cost).

## The shape of a journey

- **Kishōtenketsu** — introduce, develop, twist, conclude — is how Nintendo builds a level around one idea (Koichi
  Hayashida, interviewed by Nutt, 2012). *For class design:* levels 1–12 can be read the same way: introduce the
  mechanic, develop it, turn it, and conclude it at the peak.
- The hero's journey (Vogler; Campbell) is a **lens for texts and arcs, never a formula** for a kit.

## Clarity — reading the game at a glance

- Riot defines clarity as being able to understand what is happening and respond to it; it keeps a hierarchy inside
  each kit and a distinctive primary trait per champion (*Clarity in League*, Riot Games). *For class design:* the
  player answers the turn's question by looking at the screen (statuses, counts, combat log), and the subclass has one
  trait a stranger would name.

## Mechanics that tell the story

- *Ludonarrative dissonance* (Hocking, 2007) names a game whose rules say the opposite of its story. `27.05` holds the
  harmony questions. *For class design:* the mechanic should make the player act like the character the fantasy
  promises.

## Designing for D&D-style classes

- Jeremy Crawford (D&D Beyond, *Designing D&D Subclasses*): a subclass starts from its story; balance places it among
  the subclasses of the **same class**; rules are written for the common case at the table first. `27.02` holds the
  priority order.
- 4th Edition's four roles (Controller, Defender, Leader, Striker; *Player's Handbook*, 2008) are a usable taxonomy for
  "what does it bring to a party of four" — a vocabulary, not a requirement.
